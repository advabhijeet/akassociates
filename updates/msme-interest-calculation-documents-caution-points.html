MSME Interest Calculation: Documents and Caution Points

A practical Section 16 guide for building an interest worksheet that can be checked invoice by invoice—starting with the correct Section 15 due date, historical RBI Bank Rate periods, monthly rests, payments and documentary proof.

Practical Guide
Published 5 October 2026MSMESection 16Interest CalculationDocuments

The statutory formula is easy to state but easy to miscalculate. Section 16 requires compound interest with monthly rests at three times the RBI Bank Rate, but the calculation is only as good as the Section 15 due date, invoice balance, payment allocation and historical rate record used as inputs. A defensible claim should therefore show the methodology and documents, not merely a final interest figure.

Interest calculation in one minute

  1. Identify the correct statutory supplier and the invoices that are actually unpaid.
  2. Calculate the Section 15 due date for each invoice or milestone.
  3. Record the principal balance after credits, returns and payments.
  4. Obtain the RBI Bank Rate for every historical period in the delay.
  5. Apply three times the Bank Rate—not the repo rate, MCLR or a contractual penal rate.
  6. Apply compound interest with monthly rests, not simple interest.
  7. Reduce the interest base when legally relevant payments are made.
  8. Keep a reproducible worksheet that can be updated to a later cut-off date.

What Section 16 actually requires

Section 16 of the Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006 applies where the buyer fails to make payment as required under Section 15. The buyer is liable, notwithstanding a contrary agreement or another law, to pay compound interest with monthly rests at three times the Bank Rate notified by the Reserve Bank of India.

The Supreme Court in Sonali Power Equipments reproduced this statutory scheme and reiterated that Section 17 treats the recoverable amount as principal together with the Section 16 interest.

The detailed legal interpretation of the provision is covered in the Section 16 MSMED Act authority guide. This page focuses on building and checking the calculation record.

Do not begin with the interest rate—begin with the due date

The most common calculation error is to select an interest percentage before deciding when payment became legally overdue.

Section 16 begins from the appointed day or, where applicable, the date immediately following the payment date agreed in writing. That means Section 15 and the definitions of acceptance, deemed acceptance and appointed day must be applied first.

If the due date is wrong by twenty or thirty days, every later monthly-rest calculation is also wrong.

The invoice date is not a universal interest start date

An invoice may be issued before, on or after delivery or completion. Section 15 is concerned with payment terms and acceptance/deemed acceptance, not with a universal rule that interest begins forty-five days after the invoice date.

A worksheet that simply adds forty-five days to every invoice can therefore overstate or understate the statutory interest period.

Step 2: determine the principal actually outstanding

Interest should be calculated on the amount that is legally unpaid, not on the gross invoice total where the buyer has already made payments or where valid credits/returns are admitted.

Create an invoice ledger showing the opening invoice amount, payments, credit notes, returns, adjustments and balance outstanding.

Do not calculate Section 16 interest on an amount that the supplier's own bank statement shows was already received.

Step 3: collect payment dates from bank evidence

The supplier ledger is useful but should be checked against bank statements, UTRs, remittance advice and buyer communication. Record the actual date on which funds were received or otherwise legally credited.

Where the buyer made a lump-sum payment without identifying invoices, the allocation method should be disclosed. Avoid silently allocating payments to whichever invoice produces the highest interest figure.

Step 4: use the RBI Bank Rate—not a substitute

Section 16 expressly refers to the Bank Rate notified by the Reserve Bank of India. Do not substitute the policy repo rate, Standing Deposit Facility rate, MCLR, base rate, the supplier's borrowing rate or the contractual interest rate.

As a dated illustration, the latest official RBI current-rates page located for this update, dated 6 August 2026, listed the Bank Rate at 5.50%. Three times that rate is 16.50% per annum.

Important: 16.50% is only a dated illustration based on that RBI rate page. It is not a permanent MSME interest rate and should not be applied retrospectively to periods when the Bank Rate was different.

Rate changes should split the calculation period

If the Bank Rate changes while an invoice remains unpaid, the interest worksheet should generally reflect the different statutory rates for the different periods rather than applying the latest rate to the entire historical delay.

The exact spreadsheet implementation can vary, but the legal inputs must remain visible: effective date of the RBI rate, statutory multiplier, monthly rests and outstanding balance.

Step 6: apply monthly rests, not simple interest

Section 16 uses the expression "compound interest with monthly rests". The accrued interest therefore affects the calculation base at each monthly rest according to the compounding methodology.

A simple-interest formula such as principal × annual rate × days/365 does not reproduce monthly compounding.

If a software tool or spreadsheet is used, retain the formula logic and intermediate rows so that the Council, buyer or tribunal can audit the calculation.

Do not hide the compounding formula inside a single cell

A good worksheet should allow another person to answer three questions: what balance existed during the month, what statutory rate applied, and how the closing balance was reached.

Where the period is long, a month-by-month schedule or a rate-period schedule with transparent compounding steps is preferable to a single opaque formula.

Step 7: treat part-payments transparently

Part-payments are one of the main reasons two MSME interest calculations produce very different totals. Record every payment with date, amount and allocation.

After a payment, the worksheet should not continue charging interest on an amount that is no longer outstanding. Where allocation is disputed, show the supplier's proposed allocation and the buyer's alternative position if known.

Part-payment can also be relevant to limitation, which is a separate legal question from arithmetic.

Interest calculation and payment appropriation are different questions

The mathematical worksheet should not silently resolve a legal dispute about appropriation. If the contract, debtor or creditor has appropriated a payment to a particular invoice, that record should be reviewed.

If there is no clear appropriation, identify the assumption used and obtain legal advice where the allocation materially changes principal, interest or limitation.

Step 8: keep credit notes, returns and admitted deductions visible

Where goods were returned, a credit note was issued or the supplier expressly accepted a deduction, reflect it in the principal schedule and the date from which it affects the balance.

Do not simply delete the transaction from the spreadsheet. Keeping the adjustment visible creates an audit trail and reduces disputes about why the invoice amount differs from the amount claimed.

What about disputed debit notes?

A unilateral debit note issued by the buyer does not automatically establish that the supplier's principal was reduced. Treat disputed deductions as a liability issue, not as an arithmetic fact.

The interest worksheet can show the supplier's claimed balance while the pleading separately explains why the debit note is disputed.

Step 9: separate accrued interest from continuing interest

For filing purposes, calculate accrued Section 16 interest up to a specific cut-off date and state that date clearly.

The prayer can separately seek continuing statutory interest until payment or realisation to the extent legally permissible. This avoids presenting a moving figure as if it were fixed forever.

When the matter is later heard or an award is prepared, update the worksheet rather than manually adding an unexplained amount.

Documents that should support the interest worksheet

  1. Udyam and supplier-status records relevant to the claim.
  2. Purchase/work order or written agreement containing payment terms.
  3. Invoices.
  4. Delivery challans, GRNs, service reports or completion records.
  5. Written buyer objections concerning acceptance and the supplier's response.
  6. Supplier ledger.
  7. Bank statements, UTRs and remittance advice.
  8. Credit notes and return records.
  9. Any payment-allocation or reconciliation correspondence.
  10. Official RBI Bank Rate records for each relevant period.
  11. The spreadsheet/worksheet showing formulas and monthly rests.
  12. A short methodology note explaining assumptions and the calculation cut-off.

The methodology note is worth preparing

A one-page methodology note can prevent substantial confusion. It should identify the Section 15 due-date rule used, interest start, RBI rate sources, treatment of rate changes, monthly-rest methodology, payment allocation and cut-off date.

If the worksheet contains assumptions, disclose them. Hidden assumptions are a common source of challenge.

Worked structure—not a universal calculator

Suppose an invoice remains unpaid after the applicable Section 15 due date. The calculation process is:

  1. Identify the exact date Section 16 interest begins.
  2. Identify the outstanding principal on that date.
  3. Identify the RBI Bank Rate applicable from that date.
  4. Multiply that Bank Rate by three to obtain the statutory annual rate for the relevant period.
  5. Apply compound interest with monthly rests.
  6. Split the period when the Bank Rate changes.
  7. Reduce/reallocate the outstanding balance when payments or admitted credits occur.
  8. Continue to the stated calculation cut-off date.

This structure is more reliable than publishing a universal online percentage calculator because the due date, rate changes and payment history differ from claim to claim.

Do not confuse Bank Rate with repo rate

RBI publishes several policy rates together. The fact that Bank Rate may track another policy rate at a given time does not change the statutory language: Section 16 expressly refers to Bank Rate.

Label the source column "Bank Rate" and save the official rate record used.

Do not use the contractual interest rate instead of Section 16

Section 16 operates notwithstanding anything in an agreement between buyer and supplier or another law. A contract stating 12%, 18% or no interest at all does not automatically replace the statutory rate where Chapter V applies.

A contractual rate can still be relevant to other claims or periods outside the statutory framework, but it should not be silently substituted into a Section 16 worksheet.

Do not calculate interest before resolving supplier eligibility

A technically perfect spreadsheet does not prove entitlement to Section 16. The claimant must still fall within the statutory delayed-payment framework for the relevant transaction.

Registration timing remains legally sensitive after the Supreme Court's 2025 NBCC (India) Ltd. larger-Bench reference. Keep the registration chronology alongside the calculation record.

Limitation and interest are separate analyses

A large interest figure does not make an old principal claim enforceable. Limitation should be reviewed independently before filing.

The Supreme Court in Sonali Power Equipments distinguishes settlement-oriented conciliation from adjudicatory arbitration: a time-barred debt may still be discussed in conciliation, while the Limitation Act applies to Section 18(3) arbitration.

See the MSME limitation guide.

Interest can become larger than principal

Because Section 16 uses three times the Bank Rate and monthly compounding, long delays can produce an interest component that materially exceeds the unpaid principal.

That is not by itself a reason to cap the statutory calculation. It is, however, a reason to make the worksheet especially transparent and to verify every due date, rate period and payment before asserting the figure.

Buyer-side audit of an MSME interest claim

A buyer reviewing a supplier's interest schedule should test:

  1. Whether Section 16 applies to the claimant/transaction.
  2. Whether the Section 15 due date is correct.
  3. Whether timely acceptance objections were ignored.
  4. Whether all payments and credit notes were accounted for.
  5. Whether Bank Rate was confused with repo/MCLR/contractual rate.
  6. Whether historical rate changes were ignored.
  7. Whether the calculation is simple rather than compounded monthly.
  8. Whether interest continued on amounts already paid.
  9. Whether the same period or invoice was counted twice.
  10. Whether the underlying principal claim is within limitation.

Spreadsheet audit checks before filing

  • All invoice numbers match the annexures.
  • No payment date is later than the calculation cut-off.
  • No negative principal appears unless intentionally treated as credit.
  • Bank Rate source/effective date is recorded for every rate segment.
  • Monthly-rest formulas are consistent across invoices.
  • Rate changes do not overlap or leave gaps.
  • Payments are not omitted from one worksheet but included in another.
  • Principal total matches the pleading.
  • Interest total matches the relief section.
  • Calculation cut-off is stated consistently everywhere.

Keep the spreadsheet editable and the filed version fixed

Maintain an editable working spreadsheet for updates, but preserve the exact PDF/version filed with the Council or tribunal. Record the calculation cut-off and version date.

When updating interest later, create a new version instead of overwriting the filed calculation without an audit trail.

Rounding should be consistent

Decide how the worksheet rounds daily/monthly intermediate values and final rupee amounts. Use the same method across every invoice and disclose material rounding assumptions.

Small rounding differences may be unavoidable; unexplained large differences are not.

Interest calculation should feed the claim, not dominate it

The supplier must still prove the underlying supply, liability and principal. A 50-page interest spreadsheet cannot cure weak proof of delivery or a wrong claimant entity.

Use the MSME claim-preparation guide to build the entire case file around the calculation.

Current 2026 rate and statutory caution

Rate caution: the latest official RBI current-rates page located for this update, dated 6 August 2026, listed Bank Rate at 5.50%, giving a Section 16 multiple of 16.50% per annum for that dated illustration. Verify the Bank Rate again for the live calculation and for every historical rate period.

The official materials reviewed for this publication did not establish commencement of substituted provisions affecting this calculation framework.

Frequently asked questions

Can I simply calculate 16.5% compound interest for my entire claim?

No. 16.50% is only the three-times multiple of a 5.50% Bank Rate for a period when that Bank Rate applies. Historical Bank Rate changes and the correct Section 15 due date must be reflected.

Is monthly compounding the same as adding interest once every month?

The worksheet must implement compound interest with monthly rests consistently. It should show how accrued interest affects the calculation base at each rest rather than merely calculating simple interest in monthly blocks.

What if the buyer made several part-payments?

Record each payment by date and allocation. The outstanding base should be updated rather than charging interest on the original principal after it has been partly paid.

Can I use a commercial MSME interest calculator?

It can be used as a cross-check, but the filed claim should retain the legal inputs and calculations needed to verify due dates, historical RBI rates, payments and monthly rests. Do not rely on an unexplained web-calculator output.

Should interest be calculated up to the filing date or hearing date?

For filing, use a clearly stated cut-off date and claim continuing statutory interest as legally permissible. Update the worksheet later when required by the Council/tribunal or for settlement and award calculations.

Can the buyer challenge only the interest even if principal is admitted?

Yes. Principal liability and the correctness of the statutory interest calculation can be distinct issues. Due date, payments, Bank Rate periods and formula can be contested even where some principal is admitted.

Conclusion

The most reliable MSME interest calculation is an auditable record, not a headline percentage. Start with the Section 15 due date, establish the true unpaid balance, apply the correct historical RBI Bank Rate periods at the statutory three-times multiplier, compound with monthly rests and reflect every payment and credit transparently.

When those inputs are documented and the spreadsheet can be independently reproduced, the Section 16 figure becomes much easier to plead, negotiate, defend and adjudicate.
