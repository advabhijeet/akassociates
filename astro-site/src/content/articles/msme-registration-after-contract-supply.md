## Answer in brief

A Udyam certificate obtained after a contract was signed does not, by itself, settle whether a supplier may invoke the Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Council (MSEFC). The dates of the contract, actual supplies, registration and claim are distinct. Earlier Supreme Court judgments contain restrictive statements about retrospective benefits. A subsequent two-judge Bench in NBCC questioned whether those decisions resolved the precise issue of access to the Council and referred the matter to a three-judge Bench.

As of this research review on 27 November 2026, a conclusive later larger-Bench determination has not been verified in the available sources. Neither automatic rejection nor automatic admission should be promised. The statutory registration provisions were also amended in 2026, making it important to identify which version of the Act applies to the particular transaction.

## Separate the statutory questions

A registration-timing objection can involve at least three questions:

1. Was the enterprise actually classified as micro or small at the relevant time?
2. Was it a supplier for the purposes of Section 2(n) and the payment and interest obligations under Sections 15–17?
3. Is a reference under Section 18 maintainable, and can the particular relief claimed be granted?

These questions overlap but are not identical. The expression concerning parties to a dispute in Section 18(1) must be read with the amount due under Section 17. An eligible reference does not establish every element of the underlying claim. The definition of supplier in Section 2(n) must be read in full, including its specific inclusive categories.

The applicable registration regime must be identified separately from current classification thresholds. A certificate issued today is not a substitute for historical evidence of the nature, classification and registration of the business. The date of commencement of business appearing on a certificate must not automatically be treated as the date of registration.

## Silpi Industries: why the supply date matters

In [Silpi Industries v. Kerala State Road Transport Corporation](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/134928159/), (2021) 18 SCC 790, decided on 29 June 2021, the Supreme Court dealt with limitation and counterclaims, as well as the facts of a connected appeal involving a supplier seeking statutory benefits. The latter supplier had completed supplies before registration and did not establish later performance.

Paragraph 26 expressed a restrictive view of eligibility where the contract and supplies preceded registration. The Court distinguished the Delhi High Court decision in [GE T&D India Ltd. v. Reliable Engineering Projects and Marketing](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/77040817/) (15 February 2017, paragraphs 35–36 and 40), in which supplies continued after registration. The Delhi High Court was deciding applicability of the MSMED Act in connection with a pre-deposit application, rather than finally determining the underlying debt. This factual distinction matters when an ongoing project extends across the registration date.

Do not infer from this distinction that every invoice dated after registration represents a new eligible supply. Evidence of actual performance is necessary.

## Mahakali Foods: prospective effect and tribunal jurisdiction

In [Gujarat State Civil Supplies Corporation Ltd. v. Mahakali Foods Pvt. Ltd. (Unit 2)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/78592269/), (2023) 6 SCC 401, decided on 31 October 2022, the Court considered the relationship between the MSMED Act and the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

Paragraphs 33 and 34(vi) reiterated that a later registration does not retrospectively confer the special statutory benefits, while recognising prospective application to subsequent supplies or services. It also described the registration issue as one that could be decided by the Council or institution acting as an arbitral tribunal. Read this passage together with the actual issues in the judgment and the subsequent NBCC reference.

## NBCC: the question referred to a larger Bench

In [NBCC (India) Ltd. v. State of West Bengal](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2022/24558/24558_2022_12_1501_58501_Judgement_10-Jan-2025.pdf), 2025 INSC 54, decided on 10 January 2025, the Court analysed Section 18 and the scope of earlier decisions.

In paragraphs 1.2 and 29, the Bench expressed the view that access to Section 18 is not confined to a supplier that has filed a memorandum under Section 8. It distinguished the right to seek a reference from the question of entitlement to statutory payment benefits, and examined whether the earlier cases had actually decided the precise maintainability issue. In paragraph 29 it referred the appeal to a three-judge Bench for certainty about the applicable precedents. The earlier decisions were not formally overruled by that reference.

In [Sadbhav Infrastructure Project Ltd. v. VCS Enterprises Pvt. Ltd.](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/131396372/), order dated 18 March 2026, the Delhi High Court treated the prior-registration issue as covered by NBCC and disposed of the petition, leaving other legal issues open. This demonstrates subsequent reliance on NBCC but does not amount to a larger-Bench ruling. The status of the Supreme Court reference should still be verified before relying on a definitive proposition.

A subsequent Delhi High Court order dated 13 April 2026 in [Larsen and Toubro Ltd. v. Navnirman Highway Projects Pvt. Ltd.](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/142415491/) illustrates the continuing controversy. The Court issued notice in an award challenge and granted conditional interim stay. It did not finally decide the registration issue in that order. Do not cite an interim stay as a binding final resolution of the merits. Later orders dated 6 August and 17 September 2026 are indexed, but their full text could not be retrieved during this review; the present stay and disposal status are not established.

Check the current status of the Supreme Court reference, connected matters and later orders before relying on these authorities in a live case.

## The MSMED Amendment Act, 2026

Amendment Act No. 16 of 2026, section 5, substitutes Section 8 of the principal Act. The new text provides for free and voluntary registration through a notified national digital platform, with a corresponding provision for State platforms. Section 2 of the Amendment Act modifies several definitions but does not expressly substitute Section 2(n).

S.O. 5575(E) dated 12 October 2026 commenced Amendment Act sections 2–7, 11 and 13–17 on 15 October 2026. That includes the new Section 8. Amendment Act sections 8–10, dealing with the dispute-resolution, enforcement and award-challenge provisions, were not assigned a commencement date by that notification.

The new registration wording must be analysed alongside the supplier definition and Chapter V. Voluntary registration as a general administrative matter does not necessarily answer the distinct legal question of special delayed-payment remedies. Nor does the amendment expressly declare all historical supplies eligible merely because registration was subsequently obtained. Check transitional provisions and subsequent notifications and case law.

## Transaction scenarios: a practical evidence matrix

The following examples are hypothetical. They identify issues for examination, not predetermined outcomes.

| Chronology | Issue to examine |
| --- | --- |
| Registration predates contract and supplies | Timing objection is ordinarily weaker; verify actual supplier eligibility and other defences. |
| Contract predates registration, but all supplies follow registration | Prospective-supply argument; establish actual deliveries or performance and applicable precedent. |
| Some supplies precede registration and others follow it | Segregate claims by actual performance; identify independent orders, milestones and obligations. |
| All work concluded before registration, invoice raised later | Later billing alone does not prove later supply; substantial retrospective-benefit objection. |
| Registration follows completion and payment default | Strong timing objection under earlier restrictive authorities; consider NBCC and applicable alternative remedies. |
| Earlier EM-II or UAM record followed by Udyam migration | Verify the continuity and validity of the earlier registration rather than equating Udyam issue date with first registration. |

## How to prepare the claim or defence

Create an order-wise and invoice-wise schedule recording the contract date, each amendment, registration and classification history, actual supply or certified performance date, acceptance or objections, invoice date, due date, receipts and outstanding balance. For construction or services, running-account bills and measurement records may help distinguish the billing date from the underlying performance.

Where there are several work orders, do not assume they are all part of one indivisible contract or that separate invoices create separate contracts. Examine the underlying agreements and amendments. If a claim spans the registration date, segregate the amounts and articulate the legal basis for each period.

A supplier should disclose the actual chronology, including adverse dates, and explain any pre-existing registration. A buyer should preserve the objection at the earliest appropriate stage, while separately addressing performance, payment, limitation, interest and any properly maintainable counterclaim. Do not rely solely on a registration objection and neglect the merits of the dispute.

Where the issue arises in arbitration, examine the timing and procedure under Section 16 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act. The correct procedural remedy depends on the actual stage and governing law. A pending reference to a larger Bench does not automatically stay all MSEFC matters.

## What if the MSEFC route is unavailable?

A dispute over statutory eligibility is not necessarily a finding that the contractual debt does not exist. Depending on the contract, facts, limitation and forum, an ordinary civil or commercial recovery action or contractual arbitration may be available. Filing before one forum does not automatically suspend every limitation period in another.

Read the [forum comparison](/updates/msme-delayed-payment-vs-civil-suit-vs-arbitration.html), [MSME limitation guide](/updates/msme-limitation-period-section-18-conciliation-arbitration.html), [claim preparation guide](/updates/prepare-msme-delayed-payment-claim.html) and [buyer defence guide](/updates/msefc-notice-buyer-reply-defences-documents.html).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a supplier file after obtaining Udyam registration?

Filing capability and legal maintainability are distinct. Assess the timing of actual supplies, statutory supplier status, the applicable law and the current precedent.

### Does a post-registration invoice prove a post-registration supply?

No. Delivery, performance, acceptance and contemporaneous records must support the asserted chronology.

### Did NBCC overrule Silpi Industries and Mahakali Foods?

No. It questioned the precedential scope of the earlier decisions on the precise reference issue and referred the appeal to a larger Bench. Verify later decisions before publication or use in proceedings.

### Does the 2026 amendment resolve every old registration dispute?

No express retrospective validation of every earlier transaction appears in the registration provision itself. The applicable statute, transition and case law require examination.
