# Public article draft

## Section 15 of the MSMED Act Explained: When Must a Buyer Pay an MSE Supplier?

Section 15 of the Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006 fixes the payment timeline when a statutory supplier supplies goods or renders services to a buyer. The applicable deadline depends on whether the parties have agreed to a payment date in writing. Even where written credit terms exist, the agreed period cannot exceed forty-five days from the statutory day of acceptance or deemed acceptance.

The provision is often described only as the “MSME 45-day rule”. That description is incomplete. Section 15 contains two different payment mechanisms: one for transactions with written payment terms and another for transactions without such an agreement. The definitions of acceptance, deemed acceptance and appointed day determine when the clock begins.

## What Section 15 requires

Section 15 creates the following structure:

| Transaction position | Payment requirement |
|---|---|
| A payment date or credit period is agreed in writing | The buyer must pay on or before the agreed date, subject to the statutory ceiling of forty-five days from acceptance or deemed acceptance. |
| No payment date is agreed in writing | The buyer must pay before the appointed day. |

The forty-five-day period is therefore a maximum statutory ceiling, not the automatic due date for every invoice. If the parties agree in writing to payment within fifteen or thirty days, the buyer must comply with that shorter agreed period. If no written payment arrangement exists, the appointed-day mechanism applies instead.

## Who is a “supplier” for this framework?

The delayed-payment chapter does not apply merely because a business informally describes itself as an MSME. Under section 2(n), a supplier is principally a micro or small enterprise that has filed the required memorandum with the designated authority, together with the additional entities included in that definition.

The statutory delayed-payment protection is directed to **micro and small enterprises**. A medium enterprise is not included in the section 2(n) definition merely because the wider legislation is called the MSMED Act.

Current Ministry material states that a micro or small enterprise with valid Udyam Registration may apply through the MSME delayed-payment framework. For any claim or response, supplier status and the registration record relevant to the transaction period should be checked before calculating liability.

## Written payment terms and the forty-five-day ceiling

Where the supplier and buyer have agreed to a payment date in writing, that date ordinarily governs the section 15 obligation. The relevant writing may appear in the contract, purchase order, work order, accepted quotation or another transaction document that clearly records the payment term.

However, the proviso to section 15 states that the agreed period cannot exceed forty-five days from the day of acceptance or deemed acceptance. A clause providing sixty, ninety or more days of credit does not enlarge that statutory ceiling for the purpose of section 15.

The file should identify both:

1. the written contractual due date; and
2. the forty-five-day outer limit calculated from acceptance or deemed acceptance.

Where the written due date falls earlier, payment is required by that earlier date. Where the written term purports to exceed forty-five days, the statutory ceiling becomes material.

## No written payment term: the appointed-day rule

Where there is no written agreement fixing the payment date, section 15 requires payment before the “appointed day”. Section 2(b) defines the appointed day as the day immediately following the expiry of fifteen days from the day of acceptance or deemed acceptance.

This is why it is inaccurate to treat every transaction as automatically carrying forty-five days of credit. Without a written payment agreement, the statute uses the shorter appointed-day mechanism.

The exact calculation should be made from the proved acceptance or deemed-acceptance date. A supplier should not rely only on the invoice date where delivery or rendering of services occurred on another date.

## Day of acceptance

The Act defines the day of acceptance by reference to actual delivery of goods or rendering of services. It also deals specifically with a buyer’s written objection.

Where the buyer makes an objection **in writing** regarding acceptance within fifteen days from delivery or rendering of services, the day of acceptance becomes the day on which the supplier removes that objection.

The practical record should therefore show:

- when the goods were delivered or the services were rendered;
- whether the buyer objected in writing within fifteen days;
- the precise subject of the objection;
- how and when the supplier addressed it; and
- whether the buyer thereafter used, accepted or retained the goods or services.

An oral complaint or a general assertion raised much later should not be treated as equivalent to the written-objection mechanism without examining the complete transaction record.

## Day of deemed acceptance

Where the buyer does not make a written objection regarding acceptance within fifteen days from delivery of goods or rendering of services, the Act treats the actual delivery or service-rendering date as the day of deemed acceptance.

This makes contemporaneous correspondence important. A buyer relying on a quality, quantity, specification or completion dispute should preserve the written objection and proof of when it was sent. A supplier should preserve delivery proof, completion records and the communication trail showing whether any timely written objection was received.

## Why the invoice date may not decide the statutory timeline

The statutory definitions focus on delivery of goods, rendering of services, written objections and removal of objections. The invoice date may be relevant evidence, but it is not necessarily identical to the day of acceptance.

For goods, the file may require delivery challans, transport records, goods-receipt notes, inspection reports or acceptance emails. For services, it may require work-completion records, milestone approvals, timesheets, certificates, reports, access logs or correspondence confirming performance.

Where supplies are made in stages, each invoice or milestone may require a separate date analysis rather than one combined calculation for the entire commercial relationship.

## A practical payment-date worksheet

Prepare an invoice-wise table containing at least:

| Field | Record to verify |
|---|---|
| Invoice | Number, date, taxable value and total amount |
| Supply | Delivery date or date services were rendered |
| Acceptance | Acceptance, inspection or completion record |
| Written objection | Date, mode, contents and proof of delivery |
| Removal of objection | Corrective action and date of resolution |
| Written payment term | Contract, purchase order, work order or accepted quotation |
| Statutory outer limit | Forty-five days from acceptance or deemed acceptance, where written terms exist |
| Appointed day | Fifteen-day mechanism where no written payment term exists |
| Payments | Advance, part payment and final payment dates |
| Adjustments | Credit notes, debit notes, returns, deductions and set-off claims |
| Balance | Invoice-wise principal remaining unpaid |

This worksheet should separate principal from any interest calculation. It should also distinguish undisputed invoices from invoices affected by a documented quality, quantity, completion or reconciliation issue.

## What happens after the section 15 deadline?

Section 15 fixes the payment obligation. The succeeding provisions address the consequences of default:

- **Section 16** provides for compound interest with monthly rests at three times the bank rate notified by the Reserve Bank, beginning from the appointed day or the day immediately following the agreed payment date, as applicable.
- **Section 17** makes the buyer liable for the amount due together with the statutory interest.
- **Section 18** permits a reference concerning the amount due to the Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Council.

These consequences should be calculated and pleaded from the underlying transaction documents rather than from a single lump-sum ledger balance. Separate articles should address section 16 interest calculation and the section 18 MSEFC process in detail.

## Records the supplier should preserve

A supplier preparing a delayed-payment file should ordinarily collect:

- Udyam Registration and enterprise-status records;
- purchase orders, work orders, contracts and accepted quotations;
- invoices and an invoice-wise outstanding statement;
- delivery challans, goods-receipt records or service-completion proof;
- inspection, acceptance and milestone records;
- all buyer objections and the supplier’s corrective response;
- reminders, acknowledgements and balance confirmations;
- ledger extracts, bank statements and part-payment entries;
- credit notes, debit notes and reconciliation statements; and
- a date chart applying section 15 separately to each invoice or milestone.

## Records the buyer should preserve

A buyer reviewing potential section 15 exposure should ordinarily collect:

- the supplier’s registration and enterprise-status documents;
- the written payment term relied upon by the parties;
- delivery, inspection and acceptance records;
- written objections sent within the statutory period;
- defect, shortage, delay or incomplete-performance evidence;
- debit notes, credit notes, returns and contractual deduction records;
- payment and part-payment proof;
- correspondence concerning reconciliation or settlement; and
- an invoice-wise calculation showing admitted, disputed and paid amounts.

A general denial is not a substitute for a documented transaction analysis. Equally, supplier registration alone does not prove supply, acceptance or the amount outstanding.

## Common mistakes

Common errors in applying section 15 include:

1. treating forty-five days as the automatic payment period in every transaction;
2. ignoring a shorter written contractual due date;
3. relying only on the invoice date instead of delivery, service and acceptance evidence;
4. treating an oral or belated objection as a timely written objection without proof;
5. combining several invoices into one balance without separate dates;
6. overlooking part payments, credit notes, returns or agreed deductions;
7. assuming every medium enterprise receives the delayed-payment protection given to statutory suppliers; and
8. calculating interest before fixing the correct section 15 due date.

## Conclusion

Section 15 should be applied in a fixed sequence. First verify whether the claimant falls within the statutory supplier framework. Next identify delivery or service performance, acceptance or deemed acceptance, and any written objection raised within fifteen days. Then determine whether a payment date was agreed in writing. The written date governs subject to the forty-five-day ceiling; without written payment terms, payment is required before the appointed day.

A reliable section 15 analysis is therefore document-led and invoice-specific. The contract, supply record, acceptance trail, objections, payments and adjustments should be arranged before interest or forum strategy is assessed.
