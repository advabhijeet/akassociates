Section 16 of the MSMED Act Explained: Compound Interest on Delayed MSME Payments

Section 16 imposes a statutory interest consequence when a buyer misses the payment date fixed under Section 15. The rate is three times the RBI-notified Bank Rate and the interest compounds with monthly rests, so the calculation must be date-specific, invoice-specific and transparent about rate changes and part payments.

Legal Update
Published August 2026 Legal Update MSME Section 16 Compound Interest

Section 16 does not merely add ordinary contractual interest. It creates a statutory liability, notwithstanding contrary contract terms or another law, at three times the RBI Bank Rate with monthly rests. The first task is to identify the correct Section 15 due date; the second is to calculate interest separately for each unpaid invoice and each relevant Bank Rate period.

What Section 16 requires

Where a buyer fails to pay a statutory supplier as required by Section 15 of the Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006, Section 16 makes the buyer liable to pay compound interest with monthly rests on the unpaid amount.

Rate: three times the Bank Rate notified by the Reserve Bank of India.

Compounding: monthly rests, not simple interest.

Starting point: the appointed day or the day immediately following the agreed payment date, as applicable.

Contract override: the liability applies notwithstanding a contrary agreement or another law.

Section 17 then makes the buyer liable for the principal amount due together with the Section 16 interest. A supplier's claim should therefore separate principal from statutory interest instead of presenting one unexplained consolidated figure.

Section 15 determines when Section 16 begins

Interest cannot be calculated correctly without first determining the payment date under Section 15. Where a written payment date exists, that date governs subject to the statutory ceiling of 45 days from acceptance or deemed acceptance. Where no payment date is agreed in writing, payment must be made before the appointed day.

The appointed day is the day immediately following the expiry of 15 days from acceptance or deemed acceptance. A timely written objection regarding acceptance may alter that starting analysis because the acceptance date can shift to the date on which the objection is removed.

The detailed acceptance, deemed-acceptance and 45-day analysis is addressed separately in the Section 15 MSMED Act payment guide.

Who can claim the statutory interest?

The delayed-payment chapter protects a statutory supplier within Section 2(n), principally a micro or small enterprise that has filed the required memorandum, together with the other entities specifically included by the Act. A medium enterprise does not obtain Chapter V protection merely because the legislation is called the MSMED Act.

Registration timing must be matched with the contract and supply dates. A later registration should not be assumed to operate retrospectively for earlier supplies. Where the commercial relationship spans registration, renewals and multiple purchase orders, invoice groups may require separate eligibility review.

What does three times the RBI Bank Rate mean?

The statutory multiplier uses the RBI-notified Bank Rate. It is not automatically three times the repo rate, the supplier's lending rate, a bank's MCLR, the contractual penal rate or a rate selected for convenience.

The Bank Rate can change during a long period of default. A defensible worksheet should therefore identify the RBI notification or official rate record applicable to each part of the delay period rather than applying today's rate retrospectively to the entire claim.

Illustration dated 1 August 2026: the RBI listed the Bank Rate at 5.50%. Three times that rate is 16.50% per annum, compounded monthly.

Important: this is a dated illustration, not a permanent statutory percentage. Verify every historical rate period used in the claim.

Why monthly rests matter

Simple interest calculates interest only on principal. Compound interest with monthly rests adds the accrued interest to the calculation base at each monthly rest, so later months earn interest on the accumulated amount.

Using the dated 16.50% illustration, ₹1,00,000 outstanding for 12 complete months would produce approximately ₹17,807 in interest under a standard nominal-rate monthly-compounding illustration, giving a total of approximately ₹1,17,807. Simple interest for the same illustration would be ₹16,500. The difference grows as the period increases.

This example assumes one unchanged rate, one principal amount, no part payment and 12 complete monthly periods. Real claims frequently require segmented rates, invoice-level dates, broken periods, credit notes and payment adjustments.

A practical monthly-compounding formula

For a complete period during which one Bank Rate applies, a transparent worksheet may show:

Statutory annual rate: RBI Bank Rate × 3.

Monthly rate: statutory annual rate ÷ 12.

Accumulated amount: principal × (1 + monthly rate)number of completed monthly rests.

Interest: accumulated amount − principal.

The Act states the rate and monthly-rest requirement but does not turn every evidentiary issue into one universal spreadsheet convention. For incomplete months, changing rates, payments between rests or disputed components, disclose the exact method used and keep an alternative calculation available where the forum may adopt a different convention.

How to handle RBI Bank Rate changes

A multi-year delay may pass through several RBI Bank Rate changes. The worksheet should divide the period into rate blocks:

  1. record the Section 16 interest-start date;
  2. identify each RBI Bank Rate change after that date;
  3. apply three times the applicable rate to the corresponding block;
  4. carry forward the compounded closing balance into the next block; and
  5. retain the RBI notification or official rate record for every change.

Applying one present-day rate to an entire historical period may materially overstate or understate the claim. The calculation sheet should be reproducible from the attached rate records.

Calculate invoice by invoice

Different invoices commonly have different supply dates, acceptance dates, written payment terms and part-payment histories. One interest start date for the entire ledger is therefore often inaccurate.

Prepare one row or calculation schedule for each invoice or milestone, showing:

  • invoice number, date and principal claimed;
  • delivery or service-completion date;
  • acceptance or deemed-acceptance date;
  • written objection and removal date, if any;
  • written payment term and Section 15 ceiling;
  • Section 16 interest-start date;
  • RBI Bank Rate blocks;
  • part payments, credit notes and adjustments; and
  • interest cut-off date and closing balance.

Part payments and credit adjustments

A part payment changes the outstanding balance from its effective date, but the record must first establish which invoice or liability the payment was intended to satisfy. The buyer's remittance advice, supplier ledger, bank narration, reconciliation statement and correspondence may be relevant.

Do not silently allocate a general payment to whichever invoice maximises or minimises interest. State the allocation basis. Where allocation is disputed, provide alternative schedules so the Council or tribunal can see the financial effect of each position.

Credit notes, returns, debit notes and agreed deductions should also be dated and mapped to the affected invoice. A disputed debit note is not automatically equivalent to an admitted reduction of principal.

Which amount should carry interest?

Section 16 applies to the amount that the buyer failed to pay as required by Section 15. The claim base must therefore be reconciled with the contract, invoices, supply proof, taxes, returns, deductions and payments.

Do not assume that every ledger component, tax entry, penalty or incidental charge automatically carries statutory interest. Identify the legal and documentary basis for each component and separate admitted principal from contested additions.

Can the contract replace or reduce Section 16 interest?

Section 16 expressly operates notwithstanding anything contained in an agreement between the buyer and supplier or in another law. A clause prescribing a lower rate, simple interest only or no interest does not by itself displace the statutory consequence where Chapter V validly applies.

The contract remains relevant to determine the written payment date, scope of supply, acceptance mechanism, deductions and underlying principal. It cannot be read in isolation from Sections 15 to 17.

Section 23 tax consequence for the buyer

Section 23 provides that interest payable or paid by a buyer under the MSMED Act is not allowed as a deduction when computing income under the Income-tax Act. This statutory consequence is separate from the recent income-tax timing rules concerning unpaid amounts to micro and small enterprises.

Financial and tax teams should therefore keep the principal and Section 16 interest separately identified in the ledger, settlement terms and award-compliance record.

Annual-account disclosure and audit records

Section 22 requires specified buyers whose annual accounts are audited to disclose prescribed information concerning unpaid principal and interest. The accounts team should reconcile the vendor ledger, statutory MSE status, ageing report, disputed invoices and Section 16 calculation before finalisation.

A buyer should not wait for an MSEFC notice before identifying potential statutory interest exposure. A supplier should likewise preserve its own invoice-wise calculation and not rely only on the buyer's disclosure.

How Section 16 reaches the MSEFC

Section 18 permits a reference concerning an amount due under Section 17, which combines principal and statutory interest. The Facilitation Council process may involve conciliation and, if conciliation fails, arbitration under the statutory framework.

In Gujarat State Civil Supplies Corporation Ltd. v. Mahakali Foods Pvt. Ltd., the Supreme Court confirmed that an eligible statutory reference is not displaced merely because the contract contains an independent arbitration clause. Eligibility, registration timing, limitation, jurisdiction and the transaction record must still be proved.

The separate MSEFC process guide addresses filing, conciliation, arbitration, award and post-award stages.

Challenge to an MSEFC award and the 75% deposit

Section 19 requires a buyer challenging an MSEFC award to deposit 75% of the amount in terms of the award before the setting-aside application is entertained. The court may order release of a reasonable portion to the supplier subject to conditions.

In M/s Tirupati Steel v. M/s Shubh Industrial Components, the Supreme Court reaffirmed that the 75% pre-deposit requirement is mandatory, while recognising that the court may permit the deposit in instalments in an appropriate case.

Because Section 16 interest can materially enlarge the awarded amount, both parties should test the calculation before award rather than treating interest as a later clerical exercise.

Supplier-side evidence checklist

  • Udyam and enterprise-status records relevant to the supply period.
  • Purchase orders, work orders, contracts and accepted quotations.
  • Invoices and invoice-wise principal reconciliation.
  • Delivery, service-completion and acceptance evidence.
  • Written objections and proof of when they were removed.
  • Written payment terms and the Section 15 due-date chart.
  • RBI Bank Rate notifications or official rate records.
  • Part-payment, credit-note and adjustment records.
  • Monthly-rest calculation with formulas visible.
  • A separate statement of principal, interest and total claim.

Buyer-side review checklist

  • Whether the claimant was a statutory supplier for the relevant transaction.
  • Whether the goods or services and the principal amount are proved.
  • The correct acceptance, objection-removal and payment dates.
  • Whether the 45-day ceiling or appointed-day route applies.
  • Whether every RBI rate block is correctly sourced.
  • Whether monthly compounding has been applied consistently.
  • Whether payments and credit adjustments were allocated correctly.
  • Whether taxes, penalties or disputed charges were improperly included.
  • Limitation, jurisdiction and pending parallel proceedings.
  • The financial effect of settlement versus continued proceedings.

Common calculation mistakes

  1. Using the invoice date automatically as the interest-start date.
  2. Treating every transaction as carrying an automatic 45-day credit period.
  3. Using the repo rate, MCLR or contractual rate instead of RBI Bank Rate.
  4. Applying the current Bank Rate to the entire historical period.
  5. Calculating simple interest instead of monthly compounding.
  6. Combining all invoices under one due date.
  7. Ignoring part payments, credit notes or disputed allocation.
  8. Compounding on unexplained taxes, penalties or charges.
  9. Producing only a final total without formulas and rate sources.
  10. Assuming that later MSE registration covers every earlier supply.

Practical calculation workflow

Step 1: confirm statutory supplier eligibility for each transaction period.

Step 2: determine acceptance, deemed acceptance and the Section 15 payment date.

Step 3: identify the Section 16 start date for every invoice.

Step 4: build the RBI Bank Rate timeline and multiply each rate by three.

Step 5: apply monthly rests, showing formulas and assumptions.

Step 6: enter part payments and adjustments on their effective dates.

Step 7: reconcile principal, interest and total as of a stated cut-off date.

Step 8: cross-check the schedule against pleadings, ledger and relief claimed.

Conclusion

Section 16 creates a high statutory interest consequence because it combines a three-times Bank Rate multiplier with monthly compounding. A reliable claim or response depends less on a headline percentage and more on the accuracy of the Section 15 due date, supplier eligibility, invoice-level principal, RBI rate history and payment adjustments.

The calculation should be capable of independent reproduction. Every date, rate, payment and formula should connect to a document in the record.
